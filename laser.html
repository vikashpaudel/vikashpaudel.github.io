Photobiology & Physics

Understanding Laser and Light-Based Treatments लेजर र प्रकाश-आधारित उपचारको वैज्ञानिक परिचय

LASER is an acronym for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Unlike ordinary light bulbs or sunlight—which emit broad, scattered wavelengths—a medical laser produces a monochromatic (single wavelength), coherent (in-phase), and collimated (non-divergent) beam of light energy.

LASER (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) ले एकै तरंगदैर्ध्य (monochromatic) र केन्द्रित प्रकाश ऊर्जा उत्पन्न गर्दछ जसले वरिपरिको स्वस्थ छालालाई जोगाउँदै लक्षित तन्तुमा मात्र काम गर्दछ।

Selective Photothermolysis लेजरले कसरी काम गर्छ? (Selective Photothermolysis)

Formulated by Anderson and Parrish, selective photothermolysis explains how lasers precisely target microscopic structures without burning adjacent skin:

लेजर उपचारले विशिष्ट प्रकाश तरंगहरू प्रयोग गरेर छालाको विभिन्न तहमा रहेका क्रोमोफोर (Chromophores) लाई लक्षित गर्छ:

  • Melanin (मेलेनिन): Targeted in hair follicles, solar lentigines, and pigmented lesions.
  • Oxyhemoglobin (हेमोग्लोबिन): Targeted inside superficial blood vessels and vascular birthmarks.
  • Intracellular Water (पानी): Targeted by ablative resurfacing lasers (CO₂ and Er:YAG) to vaporize and remodel tissue.
  • Exogenous Tattoo Ink: Targeted by nanosecond (Q-switched) or picosecond acoustic waves.

Ablative vs. Nonablative & Fractional Delivery एब्लेटिभ, नन-एब्लेटिभ र फ्र्याक्शनल प्रविधि

Laser systems are categorized by how they interact with the epidermal barrier and how the beam is distributed:

  • Ablative Lasers: Vaporize the epidermis and superficial dermis (e.g., CO₂, Er:YAG) for pronounced resurfacing, requiring structured wound recovery.
  • Nonablative Lasers: Deliver controlled dermal thermal injury while leaving the outer stratum corneum intact, shortening downtime.
  • Fractional vs. Nonfractional: Fractional lasers split the beam into microscopic thermal columns (Microthermal Treatment Zones) surrounded by untreated skin bridges, accelerating healing and lowering scarring risk.

How Laser Differs from IPL लेजर र IPL बीचको भिन्नता

Patients frequently hear Intense Pulsed Light (IPL) described as a "laser," but they are physically distinct:

  • True Laser: Single specific wavelength (e.g., 1,064 nm or 755 nm) with high target specificity.
  • IPL (Not a Laser): Emits broad-spectrum polychromatic light (typically 500–1,200 nm) filtered by cut-off crystals.
  • Clinical Implication: Because broad-spectrum IPL energy is readily absorbed by epidermal melanin, it requires heightened caution in darker South Asian skin tones.

Clinical Pathway of Laser-Tissue Interaction

Stage 1

Laser Energy

Calibrated monochromatic wavelength, fluence, pulse duration & spot size emitted with epidermal cooling.

Stage 2

Target Chromophore

Selective absorption by follicular melanin, vascular hemoglobin, tissue water, or exogenous pigment particles.

Stage 3

Controlled Tissue Effect

Photothermal coagulation, vaporization, or photoacoustic shattering confined within thermal relaxation time.

Stage 4

Healing & Clinical Response

Phagocytic clearance of pigment/debris, neocollagenesis, and regulated epidermal/dermal remodeling.

🔬 Expand Technical Laser Parameters (Wavelength, Fluence, Pulse Duration, Spot Size & Cooling)
1. Wavelength (nm): Determines both chromophore absorption affinity and depth of dermal penetration (generally, longer visible/near-infrared wavelengths penetrate deeper with less epidermal melanin interference).
2. Fluence (J/cm²): Energy density delivered per unit area. Excessively high fluence causes non-specific thermal burns; sub-therapeutic fluence yields poor efficacy.
3. Pulse Duration & Thermal Relaxation Time (TRT): To confine heat strictly to the target, the laser pulse width should be equal to or shorter than the target's TRT (the time required for the target to cool by 50%).
4. Spot Size & Epidermal Cooling: Larger spot sizes reduce dermal photon scattering, while contact sapphire tips, cryogen spray, or forced cold air protect the melanin-rich epidermis in Fitzpatrick III–V skin.

Explore the five major classes of laser and energy-based platforms used in modern clinical and procedural dermatology.

Chromophore: Intracellular & Extracellular Water

Ablative Lasers: CO₂ (10,600 nm) & Er:YAG (2,940 nm)

Ablative lasers rapidly heat water within skin cells, vaporizing targeted epidermal and dermal tissue while inducing thermal collagen contraction and subsequent neocollagenesis.

  • CO₂ Laser (10,600 nm): Delivers both tissue vaporization and a surrounding zone of thermal coagulation (providing hemostasis and strong collagen remodeling). Used in fractional mode for atrophic acne scars, surgical/traumatic scars, and photoaging, and in focused continuous/ultrapulse mode for precise ablation of selected benign epidermal/dermal lesions (e.g., syringomas, xanthelasma, seborrheic keratoses, viral warts).
  • Er:YAG Laser (2,940 nm): Has a water absorption coefficient roughly 12–16 times higher than CO₂, producing shallower, more precise ablation with a narrower zone of residual thermal damage—resulting in slightly faster healing but less hemostasis.
  • Fully Ablative vs. Fractional: Fractional delivery treats only 15–35% of the skin surface per session in micro-columns, significantly reducing the risks of prolonged oozing, infection, scarring, and dyspigmentation compared to traditional full-surface ablation.
Chromophore: Melanin / Hemoglobin / Dermal Water

Nonablative Lasers: Nd:YAG (1,064 nm), Diode & Alexandrite (755 nm)

Nonablative lasers pass through the epidermis without vaporizing the surface layer, targeting deeper follicular, vascular, or dermal water structures.

  • Long-Pulsed Nd:YAG Laser (1,064 nm): Penetrates deeply with lower epidermal melanin absorption, making it a cornerstone for safe laser hair reduction and deep vascular targets in darker phototypes (Fitzpatrick IV–V).
  • Diode Lasers (800–810 nm): Widely used for laser hair reduction across Fitzpatrick II–IV (and selected V with extended pulse duration and sapphire contact cooling).
  • Alexandrite Laser (755 nm): High melanin affinity makes it effective for finer hair reduction in lighter skin tones (Fitzpatrick I–III), but carries a higher risk of epidermal burns and dyspigmentation in darkly pigmented skin.
  • Nonablative Fractional Lasers (e.g., 1,540 nm / 1,550 nm Erbium Glass): Create dermal coagulative columns beneath an intact stratum corneum for mild-to-moderate acne scars and textural refinement.
Chromophore: Melanosomes & Exogenous Tattoo Ink

Pigment-Targeting Lasers: Q-Switched & Picosecond Platforms

Because melanosomes and tattoo ink particles are microscopic, heating them slowly with millisecond pulses conducts heat into surrounding tissue and causes scarring. Pigment lasers instead use ultra-short nanosecond (Q-switched) or picosecond pulses to generate photomechanical/photoacoustic shockwaves that shatter pigment into tiny fragments cleared by macrophages.

  • Q-Switched Nd:YAG (1,064 nm & frequency-doubled 532 nm): 532 nm targets superficial epidermal lesions (freckles, solar lentigines) and red/orange tattoo ink; 1,064 nm targets deeper dermal melanocytosis (Nevus of Ota, Hori's nevus) and black/dark blue tattoo ink.
  • Picosecond Lasers (755 nm, 1,064 nm, 532 nm): Deliver pulses in trillionths of a second with stronger photoacoustic effect and reduced photothermal heat transfer.
  • Critical Clinical Warning: Pigment lasers are not universally effective for melasma and can provoke severe post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or mottled hypopigmentation (guttate leukoderma) if used aggressively or without an accurate diagnosis.
Chromophore: Intravascular Oxyhemoglobin & Deoxyhemoglobin

Vascular Lasers: Pulsed Dye Laser (PDL), KTP & Long-Pulsed Nd:YAG

Vascular lasers exploit the absorption peaks of hemoglobin (around 418 nm, 542 nm, and 577–595 nm, plus a broader infrared peak near 1,064 nm) to coagulate abnormal cutaneous blood vessels selectively.

  • Pulsed Dye Laser (PDL, 585–595 nm): Considered a reference standard for capillary malformations (port-wine stains), superficial infantile hemangiomas, facial telangiectasia, and erythematotelangiectatic rosacea. May cause temporary post-treatment bruising (purpura) lasting 7–14 days.
  • KTP Laser (532 nm): Targets superficial fine facial telangiectasias; higher epidermal melanin absorption limits its use in darker skin phototypes.
  • Long-Pulsed Nd:YAG (1,064 nm): Targets deeper, larger-caliber bluish reticular veins and nodular vascular lesions, requiring careful fluence control to avoid dermal ulceration.
Polychromatic Light & Electrical / Acoustic Energy

Light-Based (IPL) & Energy-Based Devices (RF, MNRF)

Important Distinction: Intense Pulsed Light (IPL) and Radiofrequency (RF) devices are energy-based dermatological technologies, not lasers.

  • Intense Pulsed Light (IPL, 500–1,200 nm): Uses filtered xenon flashlamps to treatdiffuse photodamage, superficial lentigines, and mild rosacea erythema primarily in lighter skin types.
  • Monopolar / Bipolar Radiofrequency (RF): Uses high-frequency alternating electrical current rather than light; because RF energy is "color-blind" (not absorbed by epidermal melanin), it heats dermal collagen bulk safely across all skin phototypes.
  • Microneedling Radiofrequency (MNRF): Delivers fractionated RF thermal energy directly into the dermis through insulated or non-insulated microneedles—frequently used for rolling/boxcar acne scars and textural remodeling in South Asian skin with lower PIH risk than aggressive ablative lasers.

Unwanted Facial or Body Hair (Hirsutism / Hypertrichosis)

Laser hair reduction targets melanin within the hair shaft and follicular bulb during the active growth (anagen) phase to achieve long-term reduction in coarse, dark terminal hairs.

Relevant Technology Categories

Long-pulsed 1,064-nm Nd:YAG laser (often preferred for darker phototypes), 800–810 nm Diode lasers, or 755-nm Alexandrite (primarily lighter phototypes).

Important Treatment Limitations

Does not provide 100% permanent removal; multiple sessions (6–8+) and periodic maintenance are required. White, grey, blonde, or fine vellus hairs respond poorly. Underlying hormonal disorders (e.g., PCOS) require concurrent medical management.

Key Risks to Discuss

Transient perifollicular edema/erythema, thermal burns or blistering if tanned or improperly cooled, post-inflammatory dyspigmentation, and paradoxical hypertrichosis (stimulated hair growth on adjacent fine-haired facial/neck zones).

Clinical Applications

Dedicated Guide to Laser Indications in Dermatology

Every dermatological indication requires a verified clinical diagnosis, realistic outcome expectations, and often a multimodal combination plan.

Indication 01

1. Laser Hair Reduction

Laser energy is absorbed by melanin in the hair shaft and conducted to the follicular bulge and dermal papilla. Because only follicles in the anagen (active growth) phase contain sufficient melanin attached to the bulb, serial sessions spaced 4 to 8 weeks apart are required.

  • Response Factors: Coarse dark hair on lighter skin responds best; anatomical site, hair thickness, and underlying endocrine conditions (such as PCOS or thyroid dysfunction) influence session counts.
  • Realistic Expectations: Achieves sustained hair reduction and finer regrowth rather than guaranteed permanent eradication of every hair; periodic maintenance sessions are common.
  • Risks: Epidermal burns, hyper/hypopigmentation, folliculitis, and paradoxical hypertrichosis (stimulation of dark hair growth when treating fine vellus hair on the jawline/neck in South Asian skin).

Indication 02

2. Laser for Acne Scars

Atrophic acne scars result from dermal collagen destruction after inflammatory acne. Active inflammatory breakouts should always be medically controlled before scheduling elective scar resurfacing.

  • Scar Morphology Matters: Boxcar and shallow rolling scars respond best to fractional ablative (CO₂, Er:YAG) or nonablative fractional lasers and microneedling RF. Deep fibrotic rolling scars require mechanical subcision first, while narrow deep ice-pick scars often require focal TCA CROSS or punch techniques.
  • Outcomes & Risks: Multimodal treatment yields gradual textural improvement (often 30–60% softening over multiple sessions), not complete scar erasure. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is a key risk in Fitzpatrick III–V skin requiring pre/post sun protection.
Acne Management Chemical Peels & TCA CROSS
Indication 03

3. Laser for Pigmentation

Appropriately diagnosed discrete benign pigmented lesions—such as solar lentigines, ephelides (freckles), café-au-lait macules, and dermal melanocytoses (e.g., Nevus of Ota, Hori's nevus)—may be treated using Q-switched or picosecond lasers.

  • Diagnostic Prerequisite: Any atypical, changing, asymmetric, or suspicious melanocytic lesion must undergo clinical/dermoscopic evaluation and histopathological biopsy to exclude melanoma or pigmented basal cell carcinoma prior to any laser procedure.
Clinical Caution: Not all pigmentation should be treated with lasers. Melasma, lichen planus pigmentosus, and active post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation may darken significantly following inappropriate laser treatment.
Indication 04

4. Laser for Melasma

Melasma is a complex, chronic, relapsing photo-aging and hormone-sensitive disorder involving hyper-functional melanocytes, basement membrane disruption, and dermal vascular/mast cell changes.

  • First-Line Therapy: Strict broad-spectrum + visible light (iron-oxide tinted) photoprotection and topical depigmenting agents (hydroquinone, triple-combination cream, azelaic acid, tranexamic acid) are foundational.
  • Secondary / Adjunctive Role: Lasers and light devices are not first-line therapy for melasma. While low-fluence Q-switched/picosecond Nd:YAG ("laser toning") is occasionally considered in refractory cases, repetitive treatments carry substantial risks of rebound hyperpigmentation and confetti-like punctate leukoderma (permanent white spots). No laser permanently cures melasma.
Indication 05

5. Laser for Skin Rejuvenation & Resurfacing

Laser photorejuvenation addresses extrinsic photoaging signs such as rough epidermal texture, fine periorbital/perioral rhytids, mottled actinic dyschromia, and enlarged pores.

  • Resurfacing vs. Nonablative Tightening: Fractional ablative resurfacing removes microscopic columns of photodamaged epidermis/dermis, whereas nonablative lasers and radiofrequency heat dermal collagen without surface ablation.
  • Realistic Scope: Lasers can refine surface texture and fine wrinkles, but they do not reverse intrinsic biological aging or replace surgical lifting for advanced soft-tissue ptosis.
Indication 06

6. Laser for Vascular Lesions

Vascular-selective lasers (585–595 nm Pulsed Dye Laser, 532 nm KTP, and 1,064 nm Long-Pulsed Nd:YAG) target intravascular oxyhemoglobin to photocoagulate unwanted blood vessels.

  • Clinical Indications: Facial telangiectasias, spider angiomas, cherry angiomas, port-wine stains (capillary malformations), superficial infantile hemangiomas, and persistent rosacea-associated facial erythema.
  • Selection Variables: Optimal wavelength and pulse width depend on vessel depth, vessel diameter, flow rate, and the patient's Fitzpatrick skin phototype.
Indication 07

7. Laser for Tattoo Removal

Q-switched (nanosecond) and picosecond lasers deliver high peak power to fracture intradermal tattoo pigment granules so lymphatic macrophages can gradually clear them.

  • Ink Color & Depth: Black and dark blue inks absorb 1,064 nm effectively and clear most predictably; red ink responds to 532 nm; green, sky-blue, and yellow inks are notably stubborn. Professional tattoos require more sessions (often 6–12+ spaced 6–8 weeks apart) than amateur tattoos.
  • Specific Risks: Complete 100% clearance cannot be guaranteed; ghosting, textural scarring, persistent hypopigmentation, allergic reactions to liberated ink antigens, and paradoxical darkening of flesh-toned, white, or iron-oxide/titanium-dioxide cosmetic tattoos (lip liner/eyebrow microblading) can occur.
Indication 08

8. Lasers in Procedural Dermatology

Focused ablative lasers (CO₂ and Er:YAG) serve as precise bloodless surgical tools for vaporizing selected benign epidermal and adnexal lesions:

  • Selected Benign Lesions: Seborrheic keratoses, dermatosis papulosa nigra (DPN), syringomas, trichoepitheliomas, xanthelasma palpebrarum, epidermal nevi, and recalcitrant viral warts.
  • Oncological Safety Rule: Clinical (and when indicated, dermoscopic) diagnosis must be definitively established first. Any lesion suspicious for cutaneous malignancy (melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma) requires surgical excision or biopsy for histopathology—never blind laser ablation.
Indication 09

9. Laser for Traumatic, Surgical Scars & Striae

Fractional ablative/nonablative lasers and vascular lasers play an adjunctive role in remodeling abnormal scar architecture:

  • Surgical & Traumatic Scars: Early erythematous or hypertrophic scars may benefit from vascular lasers (PDL) combined with fractional resurfacing or laser-assisted delivery of topical corticosteroids/5-fluorouracil.
  • Striae Distensae (Stretch Marks): Early red stretch marks (striae rubrae) respond better than mature white atrophic stretch marks (striae albae). Improvement is partial (textural blending) and varies considerably between individuals.
Indication 10

10. Emerging & Specialized Laser Applications

Dermatological laser research continues to evaluate specialized applications beyond cosmetic resurfacing:

  • Evolving Clinical Uses: 308-nm Excimer laser/light for localized vitiligo, alopecia areata, and psoriasis plaques; Laser-Assisted Drug Delivery (LADD) for keloids or actinic keratoses; and low-level photobiomodulation.
  • Evidence Perspective: Patients should clearly distinguish well-established standard indications from evolving or investigational protocols that have variable clinical evidence.

Fitzpatrick Phototypes III–V • Regional Clinical Context

Laser Treatments for Nepali and South Asian Skin Types

Nepali patients predominantly have Fitzpatrick skin phototypes III, IV, and V. While increased epidermal melanin provides natural partial protection against photoaging, it acts as a competing chromophore during laser procedures—absorbing light energy intended for deeper hair follicles, blood vessels, or dermal targets.

Competing Epidermal Melanin

Short visible wavelengths (532 nm, 694 nm Ruby, 755 nm Alexandrite) and unfiltered IPL are strongly absorbed by melanin in the basal epidermis, increasing the risk of thermal blistering, crusting, and dyspigmentation if used without extreme caution.

Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation (PIH)

Melanocytes in South Asian skin are highly labile. Excessive thermal heating or aggressive fractional density readily triggers prolonged PIH lasting months. Conservative fluences, lower treatment densities, and pre/post-treatment tyrosinase inhibitors help mitigate risk.

Hair Reduction Note: 1,064-nm Nd:YAG

Because the longer 1,064-nm Nd:YAG wavelength bypasses much of the epidermal melanin to reach deep hair bulbs, it is often considered a suitable option for darker phototypes (IV–V)—provided pulse durations, epidermal cooling, and operator expertise are properly calibrated.

High-UV Environment in Nepal

Kathmandu Valley and surrounding hills experience high ultraviolet and visible-light indices year-round. Undergoing laser treatment on recently tanned skin or neglecting SPF 50+ sunscreen post-procedure dramatically increases complication rates.

Patient Safety

Laser Treatment Safety: Benefits, Risks and Precautions

Informed consent requires understanding expected transient reactions, uncommon complications, and clinical contraindications before proceeding.

Expected Effects vs. Potential Complications

Common Expected Short-Term Effects:

  • Transient erythema (redness) and perifollicular or diffuse edema (swelling).
  • Warmth, mild stinging, or sunburn-like sensation for several hours.
  • Pinpoint micro-crusting, bronzing, or superficial flaking (after resurfacing or pigment lasers), and temporary purpura (bruising after vascular PDL).

Important Potential Complications:

  • Second-degree thermal burns,vesiculation (blistering), and erosions.
  • Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) or persistent/permanent hypopigmentation.
  • Reactivation of labial/facial herpes simplex virus (HSV) or secondary bacterial infection.
  • Acneiform eruptions, milia formation, hypertrophic scarring, or keloids.
  • Severe Ocular Injury: Retinal or corneal damage if wavelength-specific protective eyewear/corneal shields are omitted.
  • Paradoxical hypertrichosis, paradoxical darkening of cosmetic tattoos, or incomplete clinical response.

Precautions, Contraindications & Isotretinoin Evidence

Key Clinical Precautions & Contraindications:

  • Active bacterial, viral (herpes simplex), or fungal infection in the treatment area.
  • Recent suntan, sunburn, or inability to comply with post-procedure sun avoidance.
  • Undiagnosed or suspicious pigmented lesions (must be biopsied first).
  • History of keloids, hypertrophic scarring, vitiligo/psoriasis (risk of Koebner phenomenon), photosensitizing medications, or uncontrolled connective tissue disease.
  • Pregnancy (elective cosmetic laser procedures are deferred).

Contemporary Evidence on Oral Isotretinoin:

While a mandatory 6–12 month delay after stopping oral isotretinoin was historically taught for all procedures, current multi-society dermatology consensus guidelines emphasize that fully ablative laser resurfacing and mechanical dermabrasion remain contraindicated, whereas laser hair reduction, vascular lasers, nonablative lasers, and conservative fractional procedures may be safely considered in appropriate patients following individualized dermatologist risk assessment.

Peri-Procedural Care

Pre-Laser and Post-Laser Care Instructions लेजर उपचारअगाडि र पछाडिको हेरचाह

Because aftercare differs between nonablative hair reduction and ablative resurfacing, always follow the specific written protocol provided for your procedure.

Before Laser Treatment उपचारअगाडिको तयारी (Before Laser)

  1. Medical Assessment: Obtain a formal diagnosis and disclose all medications, supplements, cold sore history, and prior cosmetic procedures.
  2. Avoid Tanning: Strictly avoid intentional sun exposure or tanning for 2–4 weeks prior; use broad-spectrum SPF 50+ daily.
  3. Skincare Adjustments: Pause irritating topical retinoids, glycolic/salicylic acids, or benzoyl peroxide 3–5 days before treatment if advised.
  4. Hair Removal Specifics: For laser hair reduction, do not wax, pluck, or thread for at least 3–4 weeks prior (as this removes the target hair bulb); shave the area gently 12–24 hours before your session.
  5. Clean Skin on Procedure Day: Arrive without makeup, perfume, deodorant, or tinted sunscreen on the treatment area.

After Laser Treatment तुरुन्तै र दीर्घकालीन हेरचाह (After Laser)

  1. Cooling & Cleansing: Apply clean cool compresses as directed to soothe warmth; cleanse gently with lukewarm water and a bland soap-free cleanser.
  2. Procedure-Specific Barrier Care: Use lightweight non-comedogenic moisturizers after nonablative/hair lasers, or prescribed sterile occlusive ointments (e.g., petrolatum) and antibacterial/antiviral prophylaxis after fractional/ablative resurfacing.
  3. Strict Photoprotection: Avoid direct midday sun and apply broad-spectrum SPF 50+ sunscreen consistently once the epidermis has re-epithelialized.
  4. Hands Off: Allow micro-crusts or flaking skin to shed naturally; never pick, scratch, or scrub healing tissue.

What to Avoid & Warning Signs केबाट जोगिने र कहिले डाक्टरलाई भेट्ने?

  • Avoid hot showers, steam rooms, saunas, swimming pools, and strenuous sweating for 24–72 hours (or until crusting heals).
  • Do not apply harsh scrubs, chemical peels, retinoids, or unprescribed bleaching creams during recovery.
  • Seek Immediate Medical Review If You Notice: Increasing severe pain, spreading warmth/redness, pus or yellow honey-colored crusting, clustered vesicles/blisters, fever, or sudden dark/pale pigment patches.

Frequently Asked Questions About Laser Dermatology बारम्बार सोधिने प्रश्नहरू (FAQs)

Evidence-based answers to 20 essential clinical questions regarding laser safety, efficacy, and recovery.

Laser treatment in dermatology uses a focused, single-wavelength beam of light (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) to target specific structures in the skin—such as melanin, hemoglobin, water, or tattoo ink—while limiting collateral thermal damage to surrounding tissue.

Laser procedures can be performed safely on Nepali and South Asian skin (typically Fitzpatrick phototypes III to V) when an appropriate wavelength, conservative fluence, longer pulse duration, and proper epidermal cooling are selected by a qualified dermatologist. However, pigmented skin carries a higher baseline risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and thermal injury if treated with inappropriate devices or aggressive settings.

Laser treatment provides long-term hair reduction rather than guaranteed 100% permanent removal of every follicle. It reduces the number, thickness, and growth rate of coarse dark hairs over a series of sessions, though periodic maintenance sessions may be needed, especially in hormonally influenced areas.

Session counts depend on the clinical diagnosis, anatomical area, technology used, and individual response. Hair reduction commonly requires 6 to 8+ sessions spaced 4 to 8 weeks apart; fractional acne scar resurfacing often involves 3 to 6 sessions; and benign pigmentary or vascular concerns may require 2 to 6+ sessions.

Most patients describe nonablative or hair-reduction laser pulses as a warm pinprick or rubber-band snap. Topical anesthetic cream, chilled air, or contact sapphire cooling are used when clinically appropriate to keep discomfort manageable.

No procedure can erase deep acne scars 100%. Fractional ablative (CO₂, Er:YAG) and nonablative lasers remodel dermal collagen to soften scar edges and reduce depth, often combined with subcision or TCA CROSS for tethered or ice-pick scars.

Traditional fully ablative CO₂ laser vaporizes 100% of the treated epidermal surface, leading to prolonged oozing, healing, and higher dyspigmentation risk. Fractional CO₂ laser delivers microscopic columns of thermal ablation (microthermal treatment zones) surrounded by intact bridges of healthy skin, accelerating re-epithelialization and lowering complication rates.

The 1,064-nm Nd:YAG wavelength has lower competitive absorption by epidermal melanin and penetrates more deeply into the dermis, making it a preferred option for hair reduction and selected indications in Fitzpatrick phototypes IV–V when operated with appropriate pulse durations and cooling.

Intense Pulsed Light (IPL) is not a laser; it uses flashlamps to emit polychromatic (broad-spectrum, typically 500–1,200 nm), non-coherent light modified by cut-off filters, which requires extra caution in darker skin tones.

Expected short-term effects include redness (erythema), swelling (edema), warmth, pinpoint crusting, and bronzing/flaking. Potential complications include prolonged erythema, PIH, hypopigmentation, herpes simplex reactivation, bacterial infection, acneiform eruptions, milia, and scarring.

Recovery depends on the modality: nonablative lasers and hair reduction typically involve a few hours to 2 days of mild redness; fractional ablative resurfacing generally requires 5 to 10 days for surface crusting to resolve; and fully ablative procedures may take 2 to 4 weeks, while deeper collagen remodeling continues for 3 to 6 months.
