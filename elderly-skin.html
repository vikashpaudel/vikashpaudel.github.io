What changes as skin ages?

Ageing is natural. Skin may become thinner, drier and less resilient as its supporting proteins, oil production and repair mechanisms change. Long-term sun exposure, smoking, illness and medications may contribute further.

Skin structure: a simplified comparison

Conceptual illustration, not to scale.

Common changes

  • Dryness, itching and sensitivity
  • Reduced elasticity and easy bruising
  • Slower healing and increased skin fragility
  • Uneven pigmentation and sun-related growths
  • Thinning hair and altered nail growth

Persistent itching, pain or new lesions should not automatically be attributed to age.

Common skin conditions in older adults

These descriptions are educational, not a way to diagnose a condition from appearance alone.

01 / SKIN HEALTH

Dryness & itching

Xerosis, asteatotic eczema and chronic pruritus can cause scaling, cracks and disturbed sleep.

What helps: Use gentle cleansers and regular emollients. Persistent widespread itch may need examination, medication review and blood tests for systemic causes.

02 / SKIN HEALTH

Eczema & inflammation

Nummular, contact, seborrhoeic and venous eczema, psoriasis and intertrigo can cause itchy or inflamed patches.

What helps: Avoid irritants; treatment depends on the diagnosis and may include prescribed anti-inflammatory medicines.

03 / SKIN HEALTH

Benign growths

Seborrhoeic keratoses, solar lentigines, cherry angiomas, skin tags, sebaceous hyperplasia and white sun spots are often harmless.

What helps: New, changing, bleeding or uncertain lesions need examination before cosmetic removal.

04 / SKIN HEALTH

Infections & infestations

Ringworm, candidiasis, nail fungus, bacterial infection, shingles and scabies may be more troublesome with frailty or immunosuppression.

What helps: Keep folds dry and seek diagnosis before using medicines. Crusted scabies requires prompt assessment and infection control.

05 / SKIN HEALTH

Blistering disorders

Bullous pemphigoid may begin with severe itch before tense blisters appear. Pemphigus may produce fragile blisters or mouth erosions.

What helps: Early dermatology assessment is important; biopsy and specialized tests may be needed.

06 / SKIN HEALTH

Wounds & circulation

Venous eczema, leg ulcers, pressure injuries, diabetic foot wounds, skin tears and lymphoedema can impair healing.

What helps: Seek assessment of circulation and underlying disease. Do not start compression for an ulcer without appropriate vascular assessment.

07 / SKIN HEALTH

Skin cancer & precancer

Actinic keratosis, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, Bowen disease and melanoma may appear as persistent, growing or changing lesions.

What helps: Sun protection and timely examination are important. Dermoscopy or biopsy may be indicated.

08 / SKIN HEALTH

Hair & nails

Hair thinning, greying, brittle nails, ridged nails and thickened nails are common; fungal infection can resemble other nail disorders.

What helps: Avoid traumatic trimming; seek assessment for sudden hair loss, painful nails or suspected infection.

09 / SKIN HEALTH

Bruising & fragile skin

Age-related purpura can produce purple patches on forearms and hands. Steroids and blood-thinning medicines can contribute.

What helps: Protect skin from trauma. Unexplained extensive bruising, bleeding or new symptoms need assessment.

ABCDE: signs of a suspicious mole

A – Asymmetry · B – irregular Border · C – uneven Colour · D – Diameter (often over 6 mm, but smaller lesions can be malignant) · E – Evolution or change.

Non-pigmented lesions, a new persistent sore, or a dark streak in a nail can also require assessment. Do not wait for every ABCDE sign to appear.

Daily skin care: simple and affordable

Morning

  1. Wash gently with lukewarm water.
  2. Apply a fragrance-free cream or ointment.
  3. Use broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen on exposed skin.
  4. Wear protective clothing and seek shade.

Evening

  1. Clean gently, especially skin folds.
  2. Moisturize dry areas again.
  3. Inspect feet, wounds and new lesions.
  4. Use prescribed medicines as directed.

Bathing and moisturizer choices

Keep baths or showers brief and warm rather than hot. Avoid scrubbing, fragranced soap and unnecessary antiseptics. Pat dry and moisturize while skin is slightly damp. Petrolatum, glycerin, ceramides, dimethicone and hyaluronic acid are useful ingredients; low-strength urea can help rough skin but may sting on cracks. Expensive cosmetic products are not essential. Emollient residue on clothing and bedding can increase fire risk; keep away from flames.

Skin care in Nepal

Weather & outdoor exposure

Cold, dry winter air and indoor heating may aggravate dryness. Higher-altitude and outdoor work can increase UV exposure. Use warm layers, affordable moisturizers, shade and sunscreen.

Safe and accessible treatment

Avoid unprescribed steroid-antifungal combination creams and repeated antibiotics. A family member can help arrange consultations and keep a list of medications. Online advice may help with non-urgent concerns, but examination is sometimes essential.

Caregiver corner: dependent or bedridden adults

Prevent pressure and moisture injury

  • Inspect skin daily, including heels and sacrum; watch for persistent colour change, warmth, firmness or pain.
  • Follow an individualized repositioning plan and use suitable pressure-redistributing surfaces.
  • Clean gently after incontinence, dry carefully and use a suitable barrier preparation.
  • Avoid dragging, rubbing and massaging pressure-damaged skin.
  • Support adequate nutrition and hydration within medical restrictions.
  • Check diabetic feet daily; seek prompt care for wounds.

Common pressure-prone areas

Pressure risk varies with position, mobility and medical devices. Ankles and the back of the head may also be affected.

Medicines and elderly skin

Multiple medicines can contribute to itching, rashes, photosensitivity or bruising. Long-term corticosteroid exposure may thin the skin. Kidney and liver function can influence medication safety, and topical products can cause contact allergy.

Bring all prescriptions, supplements and creams to your appointment. Do not stop prescribed medicines without discussing them with your treating physician. Severe drug reactions require urgent medical care.

When to seek medical attention

Arrange a consultation

  • Persistent or unexplained itching
  • Recurrent rashes or infections
  • Changing moles or growths
  • Non-healing wounds or unexplained bruising
  • Progressive hair or nail problems

Seek urgent in-person assessment

  • Rapidly spreading painful redness or fever
  • New widespread blisters
  • Shingles near the eye, forehead or nose
  • Infected diabetic foot wounds or blackened skin
  • Severe skin pain or rapidly worsening ulcers

Emergency: go to the nearest emergency department

Difficulty breathing, tongue swelling, extensive skin peeling, or a rash with severe illness or painful mouth/eye involvement after a new medicine can be life-threatening. Do not wait for an online appointment.

Frequently asked questions

Why does skin become dry with age?

Reduced natural oils, altered skin barrier and environmental exposure can make skin dry and sensitive.

Is persistent itching normal?

It is common but should be evaluated if persistent, widespread, severe or disrupting sleep.

Which moisturizer is best?

A fragrance-free cream or ointment is a practical first choice. Choose one you can afford and tolerate.

How often should older adults bathe?

Individual needs vary. Use brief lukewarm bathing and gentle cleansing, with more frequent cleaning of soiled areas.

Do older people need sunscreen?

Yes. Broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, clothing and shade help reduce further UV damage.

Are age spots dangerous?

Typical solar lentigines are benign, but a new or changing spot may look similar to skin cancer.

How can I identify suspicious skin lesions?

Look for evolution, irregular shape or colour, bleeding and non-healing sores; have uncertain lesions examined.

Why do older adults bruise easily?

Skin and blood vessels become more fragile. Medicines can contribute; unexplained bruising needs assessment.

Can diabetes affect skin?

Yes. Diabetes can increase infection and wound-healing problems, particularly in the feet.

How can pressure injuries be prevented?

Regular inspection, individualized repositioning, pressure redistribution and moisture management are important.

What causes blisters?

Friction, infections, medication reactions and autoimmune conditions can all cause blisters.

How are fungal nails managed?

Diagnosis may require nail testing. Topical or oral therapy depends on severity, other medicines and medical conditions.

Is shingles dangerous?

It can cause prolonged nerve pain and serious complications, especially near the eye or in immunosuppressed people. Seek prompt care.

Can I consult online?

Yes, for suitable non-emergency concerns. Some diagnoses require in-person examination, testing or procedures.

When is a skin biopsy needed?

A dermatologist may recommend biopsy for an uncertain growth, persistent rash or suspected blistering disorder.

Can itching indicate internal illness?

Yes. Kidney, liver, thyroid, blood disorders and medications are among possible causes.

Are anti-ageing creams necessary?

No. Gentle skin care and sun protection are more important. Some active ingredients can irritate fragile skin.

How can caregivers help?

Assist with gentle washing, moisturizing, skin inspection, medication organization and access to medical care.
