Elderly Skin Care & Geriatric Dermatology

Healthy Skin, Healthy Ageing

Ageing brings natural changes to the skin, hair and nails. With appropriate skin care, early diagnosis and individualized dermatological treatment, many age-related skin problems can be effectively managed, improving comfort, confidence and quality of life.

Skin Biology

Understanding Ageing Skin

Skin ageing reflects two overlapping processes: intrinsic ageing (the natural, gradual biological changes that occur with time) and extrinsic ageing (cumulative effects of ultraviolet sunlight, dry climate, pollution, nutrition, chronic illnesses, and medications).

Natural Structural & Functional Changes

  • Reduced collagen and elastin: Leads to lower skin firmness, fine lines, and reduced skin elasticity.
  • Thinning of the epidermis and dermis: Makes skin more translucent, delicate, and prone to tears.
  • Reduced sebaceous and sweat gland activity: Produces less natural surface lipid and moisture.
  • Impaired skin barrier function & increased transepidermal water loss (TEWL): Water escapes more easily, causing persistent dryness.
  • Fragility and easy bruising: Thinning dermal support around small blood vessels causes painless purple patches (senile purpura) even after minor bumps.
  • Delayed wound healing: Slower cell turnover and reduced microcirculation extend recovery time after cuts or scrapes.

Immune, Pigment & External Influences

  • Changes in pigmentation: Uneven melanin distribution leads to flat sun spots (lentigines) alongside small pale spots on sun-exposed limbs.
  • Reduced cutaneous immune responses: Lowers local defence against fungal, viral (such as shingles), and bacterial infections, and impairs immune surveillance against abnormal cells.
  • Cumulative ultraviolet (UV) radiation: Decades of sun exposure accelerate wrinkling, roughness, and precancerous or cancerous growths.
  • Chronic diseases, nutrition & medications: Diabetes, kidney or thyroid disease, poor hydration, protein or micronutrient deficiency, and long-term medicines directly influence skin resilience.

Ageing Is Natural—Not Every Change Needs Treatment

Fine wrinkles, mild greying of hair, and harmless age-related growths are normal biological features of growing older. Geriatric dermatology focuses on comfort, barrier protection, infection prevention, and early detection of treatable skin diseases—never unnecessary cosmetic intervention.

Clinical Overview

Common Dermatological Conditions in Elderly People

Older adults experience a distinct spectrum of skin, hair, and nail conditions. Below is an educational guide to the eight major groups seen in geriatric dermatology clinics.

Category A

Dry Skin and Itching (वृद्धावस्थामा छाला चिलाउने समस्या)

  • Xerosis cutis (generalized dry, scaly skin)
  • Senile pruritus (chronic itch in older adults)
  • Asteatotic eczema (cracked, "crazy-paving" dry skin on shins)
  • Chronic generalized pruritus & systemic disease-associated itch

Typical symptoms: Roughness, flaking, tightness, fine cracks, and persistent scratching—often worse at night or in winter.

Contributing factors: Dry Kathmandu winter air, hot baths, harsh soaps, reduced skin oils, medications, and occasionally internal medical conditions such as chronic kidney disease, liver disease, thyroid disorders, iron deficiency, or hematological conditions.

Prevention & care: Lukewarm short baths, fragrance-free creams/ointments immediately after bathing, gentle cleansers, and avoiding wool directly on skin.

Dermatological management: Barrier-repair emollients, targeted anti-inflammatory topicals for asteatotic eczema, and blood tests when systemic itch is suspected.

When to consult: If itching persists beyond 2 weeks despite moisturizing, disturbs sleep, or occurs without an obvious rash.

Category B

Eczema and Inflammatory Skin Diseases

  • Nummular eczema (coin-shaped itchy, oozing or scaly plaques)
  • Contact dermatitis (allergic or irritant reactions to soaps, hair dyes, or topical creams)
  • Seborrheic dermatitis (scaly redness on scalp, eyebrows, and nasal folds)
  • Stasis dermatitis (lower-leg redness, swelling, and darkening from venous insufficiency)
  • Psoriasis & Intertrigo (inflamed skin folds under breasts, groin, or abdomen)

Typical symptoms: Red or darker-toned itchy patches, scaling, weeping, skin-fold soreness, or thickened plaques.

Contributing factors: Barrier fragility, leg vein pooling, friction/moisture in folds, and unsupervised over-the-counter creams.

Prevention & care: Fragrance-free products, keeping skin folds gently dry, leg elevation when advised, and avoiding self-prescribed steroid mixes.

Dermatological management: Accurate diagnosis (including patch testing or fungal microscopy when needed), supervised topical therapy, and emollient maintenance.

When to consult: For persistent rashes, recurrent flare-ups, oozing, or pain.

Category C

Age-Related Benign Skin Growths

  • Seborrheic keratosis ("stuck-on" waxy brown or black growths)
  • Solar lentigines (flat sun/age spots)
  • Cherry angiomas (small bright-red harmless vascular spots)
  • Skin tags (soft stalked growths on neck, armpits, or eyelids)
  • Sebaceous hyperplasia, Senile purpura & Idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis (small white spots on forearms/shins)

Typical symptoms: Usually painless, slowly developing spots or raised growths; senile purpura causes flat purple bruises on forearms/hands.

Distinguishing benign vs. suspicious: Harmless growths remain stable in shape and color, whereas suspicious lesions change rapidly, bleed, crust, or fail to heal. Do not self-diagnose based solely on photographs—some skin cancers mimic benign spots.

Prevention & care: Sun protection, protective sleeves to reduce minor trauma, and avoiding picking or cutting growths at home.

Dermatological management: Reassurance after dermoscopic check; safe clinic removal only if irritated, catching on clothing, or diagnostically uncertain.

When to consult: Whenever a spot is new, changing, bleeding, painful, or looks different from surrounding spots.

Category D

Skin Infections and Infestations

  • Dermatophytosis (ringworm / tinea) & Candidiasis (yeast infections in skin folds)
  • Onychomycosis (fungal nail thickening and discoloration)
  • Herpes zoster (shingles) (painful band-like blisters on one side of the body)
  • Bacterial skin infections (cellulitis, folliculitis, impetigo)
  • Scabies, including crusted scabies (intensely itchy mite infestation)

Why older adults are more susceptible: Natural immune ageing, diabetes, reduced mobility, impaired circulation, and immunosuppressive medications increase infection risk and can make presentations atypical (such as crusted scabies).

Prevention & care: Good blood sugar control, keeping folds and toe-webs dry, prompt wound cleansing, and avoiding steroid combination creams on fungal rashes.

Dermatological management: Targeted antifungal, antiviral (early treatment for shingles reduces nerve pain risk), antibacterial, or anti-scabies therapy tailored to kidney/liver function.

When to consult: Promptly for one-sided painful blisters (especially near the eye), spreading redness with warmth/fever, or household-wide nighttime itching.

Category E

Autoimmune and Blistering Skin Diseases

  • Bullous pemphigoid (the most common autoimmune blistering disease in older adults)
  • Pemphigus (fragile blisters and painful erosions of skin and mouth)
  • Other autoimmune blistering disorders

Typical symptoms: Persistent unexplained itching or hive-like plaques (sometimes weeks before blisters appear), followed by tense fluid-filled blisters or raw erosions.

Contributing factors: Autoimmune antibodies targeting skin adhesion proteins; occasionally triggered by certain medications.

Prevention & care: Gentle skin handling, non-stick dressings, and avoiding popping large blisters without medical guidance.

Dermatological management: Skin biopsy (including immunofluorescence when indicated), supervised topical or systemic immunomodulatory treatment, and regular monitoring for comorbidities.

When to consult: Promptly for unexplained widespread itching, new tense blisters, or painful mouth/skin erosions.

Category F

Vascular and Wound-Related Skin Conditions

  • Venous stasis dermatitis & Chronic leg ulcers
  • Diabetic foot problems (calluses, fissures, neuropathy, ulcers)
  • Pressure injuries (bedsores) & Skin tears
  • Senile purpura & Lymphedema-associated skin changes

Typical symptoms: Ankle swelling, brownish leg discoloration, slow-healing sores, fragile skin flaps after minor bumps, or numb, cracked feet.

Contributing factors: Venous insufficiency, arterial disease, diabetes, immobility, malnutrition, and fragile capillaries.

Prevention & care: Daily foot inspection, protective footwear, moisturizing dry skin, gentle transfers, and avoiding tight elastic bands.

Dermatological management: Multidisciplinary care combining dermatology, vascular assessment (such as Doppler studies before compression), diabetic care, and evidence-based wound dressings.

When to consult: For any non-healing wound lasting over 2 weeks, increasing drainage, foul odor, or any diabetic foot sore.

Category G

Precancerous and Cancerous Skin Lesions

  • Actinic keratosis (rough, scaly sun-damaged patches)
  • Bowen disease (squamous cell carcinoma in situ)
  • Basal cell carcinoma & Squamous cell carcinoma
  • Melanoma (including acral melanoma on palms, soles, and under nails)

Typical symptoms: A rough persistent scaly patch, pearly or translucent bump, non-healing bleeding sore, or changing dark spot on the skin, soles, or nail bed.

Contributing factors: Cumulative lifelong sun exposure (including high-altitude UV in Nepal), fair or sun-damaged skin, chronic non-healing scars, and immunosuppression.

Prevention & care: Daily SPF 30+ sunscreen, protective hats/clothing, and periodic skin checks.

Dermatological management: Dermoscopy, skin biopsy, surgical excision, cryotherapy, or topical field therapy—early recognition significantly improves outcomes.

When to consult: Promptly for any new, growing, bleeding, crusting, or non-healing lesion.

Category H

Hair and Nail Changes

  • Age-related hair thinning & Androgenetic alopecia
  • Hair greying (natural decline in follicular pigment cells)
  • Brittle nails & Onychorrhexis (longitudinal ridging and splitting)
  • Onychogryphosis (thickened, curved "ram's horn" toenails)
  • Fungal nail infections (onychomycosis)

Typical symptoms: Gradual hair thinning, dry fragile nails, painful thickened toenails that press into footwear, or crumbling yellow-brown nails.

Contributing factors: Genetics, reduced peripheral circulation, repeated nail trauma from tight shoes, frequent water/detergent exposure, and nutritional or thyroid factors.

Prevention & care: Gentle hair care, cotton-lined gloves for housework, moisturizing nails/cuticles, trimming nails straight across after soaking, and wearing wide-toe shoes.

Dermatological management: Trichoscopy, nutritional/thyroid evaluation where appropriate, fungal testing before prescribing nail antifungals, and safe nail care guidance.

When to consult: For painful ingrown or thickened nails (especially with diabetes), dark streaks under a nail, or sudden hair shedding.

The ABCDE Approach to Suspicious Pigmented Lesions

Check moles and dark spots—including palms, soles, and under nails—for these warning features:

A
Asymmetry

One half does not match the other half.

B
Border

Edges are irregular, notched, or blurred.

C
Colour

Multiple shades (brown, black, red, white, or blue).

D
Diameter

Larger than 6 mm (pencil eraser), though smaller lesions can be malignant.

E
Evolution

Changing in size, shape, colour, itching, or bleeding.

Important note: Smaller lesions (under 6 mm) and non-pigmented (pink, red, or skin-coloured) lesions can also be malignant. Any new, changing, or non-healing spot warrants an in-person dermatological check.

Practical Self-Care

Daily Skin Care Routine for Elderly People

Consistency with gentle, affordable basics protects ageing skin far better than complex or expensive regimens.

Morning Routine

  1. Gentle cleansing: Wash face and needed areas with lukewarm water and a mild, fragrance-free cleanser.
  2. Moisturize: Apply a generous layer of fragrance-free moisturizer while skin is slightly damp.
  3. Sunscreen: Use broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher on exposed areas (face, ears, neck, forearms, and hands).
  4. Protective clothing: Wear lightweight long sleeves and a wide-brimmed hat when outdoors.

Evening Routine

  1. Gentle cleansing: Remove dust, sweat, and sunscreen gently without harsh scrubbing or loofahs.
  2. Lock in hydration: Apply an appropriate cream or ointment-based moisturizer across dry areas (especially shins, arms, and heels).
  3. Daily skin check: Inspect skin and feet for new wounds, rashes, bruising, or changing lesions (use a mirror or ask a caregiver for hard-to-see areas).
  4. Prescribed medicines: Apply any prescribed topical treatments strictly as advised by your dermatologist.

Bathing Recommendations

  • Avoid very hot water; use lukewarm water instead.
  • Limit prolonged bathing (aim for 5–10 minutes).
  • Use mild, soap-free, fragrance-free cleansers—apply mainly to underarms, groin, and feet rather than heavily lathering dry legs.
  • Gently pat the skin dry with a soft towel; avoid vigorous rubbing.
  • Apply moisturizer within 3 minutes of bathing to seal in moisture.
  • Avoid harsh scrubs, pumice irritation, bath oils that make floors slippery, and unnecessary antiseptic soaps.

Helpful Moisturizer Ingredients

  • Ceramides: Replenish natural skin-barrier lipids.
  • Glycerin & Hyaluronic acid: Humectants that draw and hold moisture in the outer skin layer.
  • Petrolatum (petroleum jelly): Highly effective, affordable occlusive that seals in moisture overnight.
  • Dimethicone: Forms a smooth, breathable protective barrier that reduces friction.
  • Low-concentration urea (5%–10%): Softens rough, thickened, scaly skin. Note: Urea-containing products may sting when applied to cracked, fissured, or actively inflamed skin.

Plain, fragrance-free generic creams and petroleum jelly are widely available in Nepal and are often just as effective as costly imported brands.

Local Context · वृद्धवृद्धाको छालाको हेरचाह

Elderly Skin Care in Nepal

Caring for senior citizens' skin in Nepal (ज्येष्ठ नागरिकको छाला सम्बन्धी समस्या) involves unique environmental, geographic, and healthcare realities.

Environmental & Occupational Factors

  • Dry winter in Kathmandu Valley & hills: Cold, low-humidity winter air and indoor room heaters rapidly worsen xerosis, cracked heels, and winter itch (asteatotic eczema).
  • High-altitude UV exposure: Ultraviolet intensity increases at higher elevations across Nepal's hills and mountains, raising cumulative sun damage even on cool or cloudy days.
  • Agricultural & outdoor work: Many older Nepali adults remain active in farming, gardening, and household chores, increasing sun exposure, minor trauma, and contact irritation.
  • Seasonal transitions: Hot, humid monsoons in the Terai and valleys trigger fungal infections and skin-fold intertrigo, while dry winters provoke eczema flare-ups.

Healthcare Access & Practical Safeguards

  • Avoiding topical steroid combination creams: Over-the-counter triple-combination creams (steroid + antifungal + antibiotic) are widely misused in Nepal, causing thin, fragile skin, worsening fungal infections, and delayed healing.
  • Unsupervised antifungals or antibiotics: Self-medication can mask serious conditions and cause drug interactions in older adults.
  • Rural access & delayed recognition: Distance from specialists can delay care; teledermatology helps bridge initial guidance when travel is difficult.
  • Affordable, family-supported care: Plain petroleum jelly, gentle syndet bars, cotton clothing, sun hats, and active family caregiver support make long-term skin care sustainable. Avoid relying on unproven home remedies or irritating herbal pastes in place of medical assessment.
For Families & Caregivers

Skin Care for Bedridden and Dependent Elderly Patients

Older adults with limited mobility face a high risk of pressure injuries, moisture-associated skin damage, and skin tears. Proactive daily caregiver routines prevent painful complications.

Essential Caregiver Practices

  • Regular skin inspection: Check bony areas daily for persistent redness, darker discoloration (in medium-to-dark skin tones), warmth, swelling, or tenderness.
  • Individualized repositioning: Help change lying or sitting positions regularly (commonly every 2–4 hours as guided by the care team) to relieve sustained pressure.
  • Pressure-redistributing surfaces: Use high-specification foam or alternating-pressure mattresses and cushions; avoid donut-shaped rings that concentrate pressure.
  • Preventing friction & shear: Use lift sheets rather than dragging the patient across bedding; keep sheets dry and wrinkle-free.

Moisture, Foot & Nutrition Support

  • Moisture & incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD): Cleanse gently with pH-balanced, no-rinse or mild cleansers promptly after soiling, pat dry, and apply a protective zinc oxide or dimethicone barrier cream.
  • Diabetic foot inspection: Check soles, heels, and spaces between toes daily for blisters, cracks, redness, or swelling.
  • Nutrition & hydration: Support adequate protein, micronutrients, and fluids according to the patient's medical and renal/cardiac guidance.
  • Early wound reporting: Do not massage reddened bony prominences. Suspected pressure injuries, infected wounds, and diabetic foot ulcers require prompt clinical assessment.

Medication Safety

Medications and Skin Problems in Elderly Patients

Many older adults take multiple regular medicines (polypharmacy) for blood pressure, heart health, diabetes, or arthritis. Combined with age-related changes in kidney and liver processing, medications frequently affect the skin.

Common Medication-Related Skin Effects

  • Drug-induced skin eruptions: New rashes appearing days to weeks after starting a medicine.
  • Medication-associated itching: Persistent itch even without a visible rash.
  • Photosensitivity: Exaggerated sunburn or rashes on sun-exposed areas triggered by certain blood pressure, diuretic, or antibiotic medicines.
  • Easy bruising: More prominent bruising in patients taking blood thinners or antiplatelet therapy.

Topical Risks & Organ Function

  • Skin fragility from corticosteroids: Prolonged oral or potent topical steroids thin the skin and delay wound healing.
  • Allergic contact dermatitis: Sensitization to topical antibiotic ointments, antiseptics, or herbal balms.
  • Drug interactions & renal/hepatic impairment: Reduced kidney or liver clearance requires careful selection and dosing of oral dermatology medicines (such as antifungals or antihistamines).

Important Medication Safety Advice

Never stop or alter prescribed heart, diabetes, blood-thinning, or blood-pressure medicines on your own because of a skin rash or bruising. Always bring a complete list (or packets) of all current prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, and supplements to your dermatologist or treating physician.

Clinical Triage

Warning Signs: When to Seek Medical Attention

Recognizing when a skin issue needs routine specialist care, urgent evaluation, or immediate emergency treatment protects older adults from preventable complications.

Arrange a Dermatology Consultation

  • Persistent or unexplained itching
  • Recurrent skin infections
  • Persistent or spreading rashes
  • Changing moles or new skin growths
  • Non-healing wounds
  • Recurrent unexplained skin bruising
  • Progressive hair or nail changes
  • Skin problems affecting sleep or quality of life

Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

  • Rapidly spreading painful redness with fever
  • Sudden widespread blistering
  • Skin peeling following a new medication
  • Rash involving the mouth, eyes, or genitals
  • Painful shingles on the forehead, nose, or near the eye
  • Infected diabetic foot wounds
  • Blackening or necrosis of the skin
  • Severe skin pain or rapidly worsening ulcers

Seek Emergency Medical Care

  • Difficulty breathing or wheezing
  • Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue with an allergic reaction
  • Severe systemic illness, high fever, or confusion accompanying a rash
  • Extensive skin detachment or painful blistering

Call Emergency Ambulance (102 in Nepal) or go to the nearest hospital emergency department immediately. Online consultation is never a substitute for emergency medical care.
