Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)

STIs are common infections passed through vaginal, anal, or oral sexual contact—and sometimes through close skin-to-skin contact. Many cause no early symptoms, yet accurate laboratory testing and timely medical care protect both you and your partner.

Symptoms—and Why Many STIs Have None

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), most sexually transmitted infections cause mild or no noticeable symptoms at first. Waiting for pain or sores can delay diagnosis and allow an infection to pass unknowingly.

Possible Signs

Common Symptoms to Watch For

  • Unusual vaginal, penile, or anal discharge (changes in color, amount, or odor)
  • Burning, stinging, or discomfort when passing urine
  • Sores, ulcers, blisters, small bumps, or skin-colored growths in the genital, anal, or oral area
  • Persistent itching, redness, or skin irritation in the groin or genital area
  • Lower abdominal or pelvic pain, pain during sex, or unexpected bleeding between periods or after sex
  • Widespread skin rashes (including on the palms or soles), swollen groin lymph nodes, or unexplained fever
Asymptomatic Infection

Feeling Well Does Not Rule Out an STI

Infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, early syphilis, human papillomavirus (HPV), herpes simplex virus (HSV), hepatitis B, and HIV frequently remain silent for weeks, months, or longer.

Many skin conditions in the genital area—such as fungal (candidal) infections, contact dermatitis, psoriasis, or benign sebaceous glands—can look similar to STIs. A clinical exam and targeted lab tests provide a reliable answer.

Common Types of STIs & Treatment Approaches

Accurate identification matters because bacterial and parasitic STIs are curable with targeted medicines, while viral STIs can be effectively managed to protect health and reduce transmission.

Curable with Antibiotics

Bacterial STIs

  • Syphilis (Treponema pallidum): Often begins as a single painless sore (chancre) and can later cause skin rashes on the trunk, palms, or soles. Treated effectively with appropriate antibiotic therapy.
  • Gonorrhea (Neisseria gonorrhoeae): May cause painful urination or urethral/vaginal discharge, or affect the throat or rectum without symptoms. Treated with guideline-recommended antibiotics.
  • Chlamydia (Chlamydia trachomatis): Frequently asymptomatic; early antibiotic treatment prevents pelvic inflammatory disease and fertility complications.
Manageable Viral Care

Viral STIs

  • Human Papillomavirus (HPV): Certain strains cause genital warts (treated via cryotherapy, topical therapy, or minor procedures), while others require cervical screening.
  • Genital Herpes (HSV-1 / HSV-2): Causes recurring tender blisters or shallow sores; antiviral medications shorten outbreaks and reduce recurrence and transmission.
  • Molluscum Contagiosum: Poxvirus causing small, firm, smooth bumps; safely removed in clinic to prevent spread.
  • HIV & Hepatitis B: Detected via blood tests; modern antiretroviral therapy controls HIV effectively, and hepatitis B is preventable by vaccination.
Curable Parasitic Care

Parasitic & Ectoparasitic STIs

  • Trichomoniasis (Trichomonas vaginalis): A common, curable protozoal infection that may cause frothy discharge or irritation; treated with prescribed antiparasitic medication for both partners.
  • Pubic Lice (Pthirus pubis) & Scabies: Cause localized itching through close skin contact; treated with targeted medicated washes or creams and laundering bedding/clothing.

When to Seek STI Testing

Testing is a routine, responsible part of healthcare—not a reason for embarrassment. Consider scheduling an evaluation if any of the following apply:

  • You notice any genital, anal, or oral sores, bumps, rashes, unusual discharge, or pain during urination.
  • You have had condomless sex with a new partner or a partner whose STI status is unknown.
  • A current or past sexual partner informs you that they have tested positive for an STI.
  • You or your partner are planning a pregnancy or are pregnant (antenatal screening for syphilis, HIV, and hepatitis B protects both parent and baby).
  • You want routine peace-of-mind screening at the start of a new relationship.

Recent Exposure? Do Not Wait for a Routine Appointment

If you recently had condomless sex, a condom broke, or you experienced sexual assault or needle/blood exposure, time-sensitive preventive medications may be appropriate:

HIV Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) — Within 72 Hours Per WHO and Nepal National HIV Testing & Treatment Guidelines, HIV PEP must be started as soon as possible and within 72 hours of a potential HIV exposure. Visit a government hospital ART/PEP center (such as Patan Hospital or Teku Hospital) or an emergency department immediately rather than waiting for an online booking reply.
Window Periods for Accurate Lab Tests Testing too soon after an exposure can give a false-negative result because antibodies or antigens take time to appear (typically 2 weeks for gonorrhea/chlamydia, 3–6 weeks for syphilis/HIV, with follow-up testing at 3 months). A clinician will advise both immediate baseline tests and repeat testing.

Prevention & Partner Care

Completing treatment and supporting partner testing prevents reinfection and protects long-term health.

Partner Care

Why Partner Notification & Treatment Matter

  • Finish your full prescribed antibiotic or antiparasitic course even if symptoms disappear after a few days.
  • Avoid sexual contact until both you and your partner(s) have completed treatment and any sores or symptoms have resolved (typically at least 7 days after single-dose therapy).
  • Recent sexual partners should be evaluated and tested—even if they feel healthy—to prevent ping-pong reinfection.
Ongoing Protection

Evidence-Based Prevention Steps

  • Use external (male) or internal (female) condoms consistently and correctly from start to finish during vaginal, anal, and oral sex.
  • Ask about recommended vaccinations—specifically HPV vaccination (protects against genital warts and HPV-related cancers) and Hepatitis B vaccination.
  • Discuss HIV Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) if you are at ongoing risk of HIV exposure.
  • Avoid unprescribed creams or self-medication—over-the-counter steroid creams can mask symptoms and worsen fungal or bacterial lesions.
