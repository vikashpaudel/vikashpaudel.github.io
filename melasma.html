Melasma Treatment in Nepal: Expert Care for Facial Pigmentation

Understand the causes of melasma, explore evidence-based treatment options, and discover personalized dermatological care for managing persistent facial pigmentation.

What Is Melasma?

Melasma is a common, acquired pigmentation disorder that typically presents as symmetric brown or grey-brown patches on sun-exposed areas of the face. While entirely benign, it can significantly impact quality of life.

Contributing Factors

Melasma results from increased melanin production. Key influences include:

  • Ultraviolet (UV) radiation and visible light.
  • Hormonal changes (pregnancy, oral contraceptives).
  • Genetic susceptibility and family history.
  • Skin inflammation or irritation from inappropriate cosmetics.

Clinical Patterns

These patterns describe anatomical distribution, not severity:

  • Centrofacial: Forehead, cheeks, nose, and upper lip (most common).
  • Malar: Primarily on the cheeks and nose.
  • Mandibular: Along the jawline.

A Chronic Condition

It is important to understand that melasma is chronic and recurrent. While there is no permanent "cure," consistent, evidence-based dermatological care can successfully manage the pigmentation and minimize relapses.

Melasma in Nepalese & South Asian Skin

Managing melasma in Fitzpatrick skin phototypes III–V (common in Nepal) presents specific practical challenges that require careful, individualized dermatological supervision:

  • Risk of Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation (PIH): Darker skin types are highly prone to darkening following irritation from aggressive treatments like harsh peels or uncalibrated lasers.
  • Unsupervised Steroid Use: A major challenge in Nepal is the unsupervised use of potent topical corticosteroid combinations available over-the-counter, which can cause irreversible skin thinning and worsening pigmentation over time.
  • Recurrent Sun Exposure: Environmental and occupational sunlight exposure in regions like Kathmandu and Lalitpur necessitates rigorous photoprotection habits.
  • Safe Lightening Agents: Ensuring the safe duration and tapering of agents like hydroquinone to prevent complications such as exogenous ochronosis.

Evidence-Based Treatment Options

Treatment is tailored to your skin type, medical history, and previous interventions. A combination approach usually yields the best outcomes.

1. Strict Photoprotection

The non-negotiable foundation of melasma management.

  • Broad-spectrum SPF 50+ sunscreen with high UVA protection.
  • Tinted sunscreens containing iron oxides to block visible light.
  • Reapplication every 2 hours during outdoor exposure.
  • Physical protection: Wide-brimmed hats and seeking shade.

2. Topical Treatments

Dermatologist-supervised depigmenting agents:

  • Hydroquinone (2–4%): The gold standard, requiring supervised duration and monitoring.
  • Triple-Combination Therapy: (Hydroquinone, tretinoin, corticosteroid) highly effective but strictly limited in duration.
  • Non-hydroquinone options: Azelaic acid, topical retinoids, kojic acid, cysteamine, tranexamic acid, and thiamidol.

3. Oral Treatment

Carefully selected systemic options for resistant cases:

  • Oral Tranexamic Acid: Often used off-label for resistant melasma.
  • Requires Medical Screening: Must not be self-prescribed. Requires thorough assessment of thromboembolic risks, contraindications, and hormonal medication use.
  • Requires individualized follow-up to monitor safety and efficacy.

4. Procedural Adjuncts

For refractory pigmentation, used alongside topicals and photoprotection:

  • Chemical Peels: Superficial peels (e.g., glycolic acid) require careful patient selection to avoid PIH.
  • Laser & Light Therapy: Not routinely first-line. Selected strictly based on clinical evaluation as rebound pigmentation and worsening are possible, especially in darker skin types.

Your Melasma Treatment Journey

Visible improvement typically requires weeks to months, and outcomes differ between individuals.

Step 1: Dermatological Assessment

Detailed evaluation of clinical features, triggers, previous treatments, potential mimickers, and relevant medical history to confirm the diagnosis.

Step 2: Personalized Treatment Planning

Selection of appropriate topical treatments, stringent photoprotection strategy, and discussion of selective adjunctive therapies based on your skin type.

Step 3: Monitoring and Adjustment

Follow-up to assess medication tolerability, patient adherence, pigmentation response, and to modify the regimen to prevent adverse effects.

Step 4: Long-Term Maintenance

Transitioning off potent treatments while emphasizing continued sun protection, a gentle skincare routine, and proactive relapse management.

Essential Patient Education

Common Melasma Mistakes

  • Applying potent topical steroids without prescription.
  • Switching repeatedly between multiple bleaching creams.
  • Overusing physical scrubs or exfoliating acids.
  • Expecting immediate, overnight results.
  • Discontinuing sunscreen after patches lighten.
  • Seeking aggressive laser procedures without prior medical assessment.

Daily Skincare Guidance

Note: Product choice and layering must be individualized.

Morning:
Gentle cleanser → Suitable moisturizer → Prescribed morning treatment (if any) → Broad-spectrum tinted sunscreen.

Evening:
Gentle cleanser → Prescribed pigmentation treatment (e.g., retinoid/hydroquinone) → Moisturizer as appropriate.

Melasma During Pregnancy

Facial pigmentation ("chloasma" or "mask of pregnancy") frequently develops or worsens during pregnancy due to hormonal shifts.

Safety first: Emphasize suitable photoprotection and pregnancy-compatible skincare (e.g., azelaic acid). Oral tranexamic acid, topical retinoids, and hydroquinone are not routinely recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
